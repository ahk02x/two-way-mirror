# FTC and New Jersey v. VIZIO (2017)

**The case that establishes this is real, in a court, with a penalty.**

- **Filed:** 6 February 2017
- **Court:** United States District Court for the District of New Jersey,
  Case No. **2:17-cv-00758** (SRC-CLW). FTC Matter No. 1623024. The consent decree was signed on
  14 February 2017.
- **Plaintiffs:** Federal Trade Commission, and the State of New Jersey / New Jersey Division of
  Consumer Affairs
- **Defendants:** VIZIO, Inc. and VIZIO Inscape Services, LLC
- **Outcome:** Stipulated order, **$2.2 million payable**: $1.5 million to the FTC and $1 million to
  the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs, **of which $300,000 is suspended** on condition that
  VIZIO complies with the order. No admission of liability, which is standard in FTC settlements.
- **If you see "$2.5 million" quoted elsewhere, both figures are right.** New Jersey's own
  announcement uses the unsuspended total ($1.5M + $1M); the FTC's uses the amount actually payable
  ($1.5M + $700K). Same settlement.

## What the complaint alleged

- VIZIO collected **second-by-second viewing data** from approximately **11 million** televisions.
- The data covered **all sources**: cable, broadcast, streaming apps, DVD, and **devices connected
  over HDMI** — meaning a games console or a laptop, not only television programming.
- The feature was **turned on by default**, and was **added by firmware update to televisions
  already sold**, whose owners had bought a TV that did not have it.
- Viewing data was **matched to IP address and then appended with demographic information** obtained
  from third parties — including sex, age, income, marital status, household size, education level,
  home ownership and home value.
- The combined data was **sold to third parties** for audience measurement and for targeting
  advertising to other devices in the same household.

## What the order required

- Prominent disclosure of the data collection, separate from any privacy policy or terms of service
- **Affirmative express consent** — opt-in, not opt-out, not bundled
- Deletion of data collected before 1 March 2016
- A privacy programme subject to biennial third-party assessment for 20 years

## Why it matters beyond VIZIO

**Count I of the complaint alleged that granular — household or individual — television viewing
activity is *sensitive information*, and that sharing it without consent causes substantial injury
under Section 5(n) of the FTC Act.** That was the first time the FTC had made that allegation. Until
then the agency's list of sensitive categories ran to financial information, health information,
Social Security numbers, information about children, and precise geolocation.

That framing is the reason every other manufacturer now shows you a consent screen. They did not
become principled. They read this case.

**It was not unanimous reasoning, and the disagreement is worth knowing.** Acting Chairman
Maureen K. Ohlhausen filed a
[concurring statement](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/1070773/vizio_concurring_statement_of_chairman_ohlhausen_2-6-17.pdf)
supporting Count II — the deception count — while writing separately to question Count I. Her
argument: "under our statute, we cannot find a practice unfair based primarily on public policy.
Instead, we must determine whether the practice causes substantial injury that is not reasonably
avoidable by the consumer and is not outweighed by benefits to competition or consumers." She
concluded that the case "demonstrates the need for the FTC to examine more rigorously what
constitutes 'substantial injury' in the context of information about consumers."

So: the sensitive-information theory is the Commission's, in a filed complaint that VIZIO settled —
not a settled point of law, and one the Acting Chairman flagged as needing better grounding. Cite it
as what it is. It is strong enough without being overstated.

**What this case does not establish:** that other manufacturers did the same thing without consent.
Post-2017, the industry moved to consent screens — which is why this repo is largely about finding
and reversing consent you technically gave, rather than about undisclosed collection.
